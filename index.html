El Niño 2026–2027: strong signal, unequal local risks

Executive brief — in plain English

Assessment

El Niño is already established and exceptionally strong. The equatorial Pacific is unusually warm, atmospheric winds and rainfall have shifted, and all six requested forecasting institutions expect the event to remain influential into early 2027. The strongest shared forecast is for a peak near November–December 2026, followed by gradual weakening—not an abrupt end. [1–6]

The key risk is not the same everywhere. Northern Brazil and parts of the Amazon are more exposed to a combination of rainfall deficits, heat, vegetation dryness, and navigation constraints. Southern Brazil faces a contrasting wet-season and flood signal. Peru’s coastal areas face increasingly wet conditions while parts of its Amazon are forecast near or below normal rainfall. These are regional probability shifts, not guarantees of floods, fires, or colony losses. [7–11]

For the Fund: a credible, time-limited case exists for financing localized preparedness and field monitoring before seasonal risks intensify. Donors should fund readiness, not a falsely certain disaster prediction. [8, 9, 12]

What changed since 9 October?

  • New IRI analysis (19 Oct): traditional Niño-3.4 weekly reading +3.3°C (centered 14 Oct); its 27-model plume places the approximate three-month peak near +3.5°C. This is not directly interchangeable with NOAA’s +2.1°C figure from 8 Oct. [1, 2]
  • New WMO outlook (21 Oct): multi-model +3.8°C forecast for November 2026–January 2027; this replaces the former October–December projection as the newest seasonal window, not as a new observed temperature. [3, 4]
  • New Copernicus (10 Oct) and Australia (13 Oct) outlooks both favor a late-2026 peak. C3S flags disagreement on peak amplitude and a fairly steep decline afterwards. [5, 6]
  • New Brazil and Peru risk evidence: ANA’s 19 Oct release reports drought across 75% of Brazilian territory in September versus 67% in August. Peru ENFEN issued revised coastal and river projections on 16 Oct. The drought percentage is not an estimate of El Niño-attributable drought. [9–11]
  • No new monthly NOAA diagnostic has appeared since 8 Oct; Brazil’s latest interagency El Niño Panel remains No. 4 (1 Oct). The quarterly WMO El Niño/La Niña update has not been replaced since September; its 21 Oct global seasonal forecast is newer. [1, 3, 7, 14]

ENSO measures an ocean–atmosphere climate pattern. Niño-3.4 is the warming of a defined central/eastern equatorial-Pacific zone. A relative index (RONI or a relative Niño-3.4 formulation) subtracts wider tropical warming to better isolate the ENSO signal. Index baselines, datasets, and averaging windows differ across agencies. +2.1, +2.81, +3.3, and +3.8°C are not a valid single time series. [1–6]

2. Probability and disagreement, explained

Persistence

IRI’s October model-based probabilities round to 100% for El Niño from OND 2026 through MAM 2027, falling to 92% in April–June and 60% in May–July. “100%” is a rounded output of a model procedure, not a literal guarantee. NOAA’s official October discussion separately states that strong-to-very-strong conditions remain above an 83% chance through January–March. [1, 2]

Peak strength

Copernicus’ 10 October multi-system analysis confirms a likely peak around the turn of the year, with systems disagreeing on maximum magnitude. IRI’s 27 models agree on very strong conditions but differ on peak heights; WMO’s newer three-month mean is +3.8°C. The independent agreement is more persuasive than any individual peak estimate. [2] [3] [5]

Spring and beyond

C3S favors a relatively rapid decline after the peak, but continued El Niño conditions through its forecast horizon. IRI still favors El Niño in spring and sees ENSO-neutral becoming most likely by June–August 2027. These are compatible possibilities; neither supports claiming that harmful local effects end the moment Pacific indices start falling. [2] [5]

4. Critical Brazil observations — what they do and do not prove

Brazil’s ANA Monitor de Secas, released 19 October, assessed September drought over 75% of Brazil, up from 67% in August. Severity worsened in 14 states, including Acre, Amazonas, Amapá, Pará, and Roraima; all three southern states remained drought-free. Acre’s severe drought extent rose from 37% to 53% of its area. These are monitoring classifications of accumulated conditions, not proof that El Niño caused each affected area. [9]

The INPE-led September Panel is careful: current Amazon hydrology is heterogeneous; it does not identify a basin-wide extreme low-water emergency like 2023–24. It calls for closer attention in Branco/Negro basins. The same panel’s seasonal CENSIPAM outlook forecasts lower precipitation in much of northern Amazonia and notes possible delayed rains in far-northern areas. [8]

5. Stingless bees and forest-dependent communities

Observed scientific foundation

Stingless-bee species differ in their thermal tolerance, forage patterns, and ability to survive dry periods. An open university-hosted field study of Melipona subnitida in Brazil’s Caatinga found that colonies use diverse native flowering plants across seasons, including dry periods. It supports protecting local floral resources—but does not measure 2026 El Niño effects in Amazonian Melipona or Tetragonisca. [13]

Reasonable conditional pathways

High heat can affect foraging schedules and nesting conditions; altered rainfall may shift flowering and the availability of nectar and pollen; smoke and low river levels may interrupt field access and livelihoods. Because rainfall can be localized and species differ, none of these constitutes a demonstrated El Niño-caused bee mortality forecast. [8] [13]

Recommended low-regret field evidence, October–March

  • Community observations: simple weekly logs for colony activity, flowering plants, available forage, and local weather, with informed consent.
  • Habitat measures: locally approved shade, firebreak, and native-flowering-plant protection; avoid habitat manipulation without ecological review.
  • Access and water: practical monitoring of community water reliability, smoke alerts, and navigability, using Brazil and Peru’s official bulletins.
  • Documentation: record baseline photos and field notes before assistance and follow up afterwards; report both success and non-results.

Five donor-facing talking points — cleared for evidence-grounded use

  1. “El Niño is already strong. The world’s leading forecast centers expect its influence to continue into early 2027; the locations and severity of impacts will vary.”
  2. “In September, Brazil’s drought monitor classified 75% of the country as affected by drought. That is an observed monitoring result—not proof that El Niño caused every drought.”
  3. “Official Brazilian outlooks identify elevated dry-season concern in parts of northern Amazonia, while southern Brazil faces a different, wetter risk pattern.”
  4. “Stingless bees depend on healthy, diverse flowering habitats. We are preparing to document local conditions and support community-defined adaptation, not claiming predicted mass bee deaths.”
  5. “Forecasts buy time. Support now can finance practical monitoring, habitat care, and community preparedness before the peak-risk months.”

Social-post controls

Always label “observed,” “forecast,” and “possible”; date every statistic; name the specific region; and link to the original public release. Never say “guaranteed floods,” “the worst ever” as an observed outcome, or “El Niño killed bees” without field evidence. Use WMO/NOAA maps only with source labeling and applicable credit. WMO does not endorse the phrase “Super El Niño.” [1–5] [14]
